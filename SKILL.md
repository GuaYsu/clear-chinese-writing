# Clear Chinese Writing

Use this skill to draft or revise Chinese prose with clear logic, natural phrasing, and high information density. The goal is not to "hide AI" mechanically, and it is not to minimize word count. The goal is to express the intended meaning in Chinese that feels coherent, idiomatic, and proportionate to the context.

## Priority order

1. Preserve factual meaning, technical accuracy, user intent, required evidence, and necessary caveats.
2. Make the logic explicit through content rather than template phrases.
3. Prefer idiomatic, conventional Chinese collocation and sentence shape over semantic calques, over-compression, or source-language framing.
4. When a sentence is understandable but still feels non-native, reconstruct it from the intended meaning rather than repairing it word by word.
5. Keep the register and narrative stance coherent within the same piece of writing.
6. Remove formulaic language only when doing so does not lose meaning, rhythm, or necessary connective structure.

Never delete a limitation, uncertainty, safety warning, legal qualification, or methodological caveat merely because it sounds formal. Rewrite it concretely instead.

## Core rules

### 1. State the positive content first

Prefer saying what was done, observed, or concluded. Do not habitually define a point by excluding imagined alternatives.

Avoid automatic patterns such as:
- “不是……而是……”
- “并非……而是……”
- “不能代表……”
- “不意味着……”
- “不使用……”
- “与其说……不如说……”

These forms are allowed when there is a real contrast, a plausible misunderstanding, a necessary scope boundary, or an explicit alternative under discussion. Otherwise, state the intended content directly.

### 2. Do not invent an opponent

Do not create a weak or nonexistent interpretation merely to reject it and make the main point sound stronger. If the reader has not been given reason to think A, do not write “并非 A，而是 B” by default. Write B.

### 3. Make rigor concrete

Rigor should come from specific conditions, data, assumptions, ranges, causal links, sources, and evidence. Do not substitute generic caution for precise limitation.

Prefer a concrete boundary such as “该结论适用于 20–60 ℃、常压条件” over a generic sentence such as “该结果不能代表所有实际情况”.

### 4. Do not confuse concision with compression

Concise Chinese is not necessarily the shortest possible Chinese. Keep words that carry comparison, stance, rhythm, or a natural relation between clauses when removing them would make the sentence abrupt or translation-like.

Do not compress a complete judgment into shorthand merely because the shorter form is grammatical. In discussion or explanatory prose, a sentence such as “相比之下，以 STE 作为出发点会更稳妥一些” is often more natural than “STE 的出发点更稳”.

Be cautious with compressed patterns such as “思路更稳、方案更轻、路径更干净、表达更硬、结构更顺” when the adjective is being used as vague shorthand. These forms can be natural in conversation or professional jargon, but should not become the default. When the judgment matters, state the relation or reason clearly.

When brevity conflicts with idiomatic Chinese, prefer idiomatic Chinese.

### 5. Keep the register coherent

Choose a register that fits the task and maintain it unless there is a reason to shift.

- In discussion, explanation, or collaborative reasoning, moderate first-person phrasing such as “我更倾向于”“我觉得这里……” can make the stance clear and natural.
- In reports, academic prose, and finished formal artifacts, use a consistent formal written register unless the genre calls for first person.
- Do not alternate casually between personal conversational language and cold manual-like declarations within the same stretch of prose.

Do not add first-person markers merely to sound human. Use them only when the sentence genuinely expresses a judgment, preference, uncertainty, or conversational stance.

### 6. Choose Chinese by intended meaning, not by cross-language word overlap

When a phrase is understandable but feels translated, identify the exact intended meaning before choosing the Chinese wording. Do not mechanically map a broad foreign-language adjective or abstract concept onto one Chinese word across different senses.

Judge collocations as units: verb-object, adjective-noun, adverb-verb, and subject-predicate combinations should be conventional for the intended register. If uncertainty concerns native collocation or sentence shape, read `references/native-collocation.md`.

### 7. Reconstruct the sentence when local repair is not enough

When a sentence is grammatically valid but still sounds translated, stiff, or assembled from abstract slots, do not limit revision to replacing one suspicious word. Re-express the intended meaning as a native Chinese sentence.

Preserve the claim, scope, and evidence, but allow changes to word order, clause boundaries, verb choice, comparison structure, and connective phrasing. A fluent rewrite may be structurally different from the source.

This is especially important when the problem comes from a whole phrase such as an unnatural predicate, an English-like noun phrase, or a compressed evaluative frame.

### 8. Keep functional redundancy and sentence rhythm

Not every word must contribute a new fact. Chinese prose can legitimately use comparison frames, modal softening, paired conjunctions, cadence, or a short transitional phrase to make a relation feel complete.

Do not remove “相比之下、不过、固然……却……、从这个角度看、换句话说” merely because the factual content survives without them. Keep them when they clarify stance, contrast, progression, or rhythm.

At the same time, do not decorate every paragraph with such structures. Their value comes from fitting the local relation, not from making the prose sound polished.

### 9. Avoid over-polished editorial Chinese

Removing AI-report style can produce another recognizable style: overly polished commentary filled with words such as “固然、绝非、归根结底、尤为、着眼于、精准、颇具”. These expressions are valid Chinese, but repeated use can make the prose sound performatively refined.

Prefer them only when they fit the register and emphasis. Plain wording is usually better when it carries the same meaning.

### 10. Keep one concept under one stable name

Do not vary terminology merely to avoid repetition. In technical and academic prose, terminological consistency is more important than lexical variety.

### 11. Let verbs do the work

Prefer direct verbs over shells such as “进行、开展、实现、完成、加以 + 抽象名词” when the direct verb is natural.

Do not over-apply this rule when the direct-verb rewrite produces clipped, unnatural Chinese. Natural collocation has priority over mechanical shortening.

### 12. Evidence before evaluation

Do not use “显著、充分、有效、重要、良好、深入、完善”等评价词 to replace evidence. When possible, give the data, comparison, mechanism, or criterion first. Use evaluative terms only when their basis is clear.

### 13. Reduce metadiscourse and ceremonial transitions

Use phrases such as “值得注意的是、需要指出的是、事实上、此外、综上所述、由此可见” only when they perform a real logical function. Do not use them as decorative glue.

Do not remove ordinary connective phrasing merely because it is not new factual content. Natural Chinese sometimes needs a small amount of framing to make the relation between ideas easy to follow.

### 14. Do not manufacture escalation

Avoid habitual “不仅……而且……”“不只是……更是……” structures that create an artificial hierarchy. If there are two effects, state the two effects directly unless the escalation itself matters.

### 15. Do not force significance

Do not automatically end sections with “奠定基础、提供参考、具有重要意义、推动……发展”. State downstream value only when it follows from the work and can be described concretely.

### 16. Structure follows content

Do not force three-part structures, symmetrical section lengths, pros-and-cons pairs, or a conclusion sentence at the end of every paragraph. A simple point can stay simple.

### 17. Remove universally true but uninformative disclaimers

Generic statements such as “实际情况更加复杂”“可能受到多种因素影响”“任何模型都有局限性”“仍需进一步研究” are weak unless the relevant factor, limitation, or next step is specified.

### 18. Use normal paragraph density in finished prose

In formal writing, paragraphs should correspond to complete logical units. Do not put every sentence on a new line. Avoid chat-like fragmentation, excessive headings, and single-sentence paragraph chains unless the genre genuinely benefits from them.

For ordinary chat replies, shorter paragraphs are acceptable for screen readability. Do not copy that layout into a report or other finished prose artifact.

### 19. Preserve voice without imitating mannerisms

When revising user-written text, preserve recurring choices that appear intentional: degree of formality, sentence rhythm, preferred level of explicitness, and use of first person. Do not overwrite these merely to conform to a generic “human” style.

At the same time, do not mechanically reproduce fillers, typos, habitual repetitions, or idiosyncratic phrases just to imitate the user. The target is writing the user is comfortable owning, not a caricature of the user's speech.

## Drafting workflow

When drafting from scratch:

1. Identify the actual claims, actions, evidence, and necessary limitations.
2. Choose the register: conversational discussion, explanatory prose, or finished formal writing.
3. Write the content directly, but keep natural connective and comparative phrasing where Chinese needs it.
4. Group sentences by logical unit, not by visual rhythm.
5. Add contrast or caveats only where the content requires them.
6. Read the result once for idiomatic Chinese: check collocation, sentence shape, pronoun repetition, long “的” modifiers, connective density, and possible semantic calques.
7. If a sentence remains awkward after local edits, reconstruct it from the intended meaning instead of continuing word-by-word substitution.
8. Read the paragraph aloud mentally for cadence: keep useful connective or modal phrasing, but remove ornamental polish that does not fit the register.
9. Run the self-check below before finalizing.

When revising existing text:

1. Preserve all substantive information first.
2. Identify the author's existing register and keep it coherent.
3. Remove or rewrite formulaic framing that adds no information.
4. Replace vague limitations with concrete scope or reasons when the source supports them.
5. Normalize terminology.
6. Repair clipped, translated, or over-compressed phrasing even if it is technically concise.
7. Merge fragmented one-sentence paragraphs when they belong to one logical unit.
8. Do not flatten the user's genuine voice into sterile technical prose.

## Self-check

Before returning finished Chinese prose, check:

- Does any sentence answer a question the reader never asked?
- Did I invent an alternative merely to reject it?
- Is any “不能代表 / 不意味着 / 并非” sentence necessary and specific?
- Did I shorten a sentence past the point where it sounds like natural Chinese?
- Did I remove a comparison frame, connective phrase, or stance marker that the sentence actually needs?
- Does any phrase sound like compressed English or meeting-note shorthand rather than idiomatic Chinese?
- Am I repairing an awkward sentence word by word when it would be better to reconstruct the whole clause in Chinese?
- Did I delete useful comparison, contrast, cadence, or modal phrasing merely because it added no new factual content?
- Did I replace one AI style with an over-polished editorial style?
- Did I choose a Chinese word because it matches a broad foreign-language word, or because it fits the exact intended sense and normal Chinese collocation?
- Am I repeating explicit subjects/pronouns, long “的” modifiers, connectives, nominalizations, or passive structures more often than the Chinese discourse needs?
- Does the same paragraph unexpectedly switch between personal conversation and manual-like exposition?
- Can a nominalized expression become a direct verb without making the sentence clipped?
- Does an evaluative adjective have evidence behind it?
- Did I add a summary, significance claim, or future-work sentence only because the section seemed to need an ending?
- Are repeated technical concepts named consistently?
- Are paragraph breaks based on changes in thought rather than a desire to make the page look easy to scan?
- If I delete a sentence, what information or rhetorical function is lost? If almost nothing is lost, delete or merge it.

## Output behavior

Apply the style silently unless the user asks for commentary on the editing choices. Do not announce that text has been “de-AI-ed”. Do not mechanically eliminate every instance of a flagged construction. Natural Chinese may legitimately use contrast, negation, repetition, first person, connective phrasing, or short paragraphs when the content and register call for them.
