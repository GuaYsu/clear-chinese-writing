# Native Chinese collocation and sentence-shape calibration

Use this reference when Chinese is grammatically correct but still feels translated, semantically calqued, or unlike ordinary native prose. These are diagnostic rules, not bans. Modern Chinese has absorbed many Europeanized constructions, and some are useful in technical or academic writing. Revise only when the current wording is less natural, less clear, or more cumbersome than an idiomatic alternative.

## 1. Choose the Chinese expression from the intended sense, not from a source-language word

Do not map a broad English word to one Chinese word and reuse it across all senses. First identify what the sentence actually means, then choose the Chinese expression that normally carries that meaning.

Example:
- “表达更干净” may mean “表达更清晰”, “语言更简练”, “行文更干练”, or “删掉了多余成分”.
- Do not decide that `clean = 干净` or `clear = 清晰` mechanically. Resolve the intended sense first.

The same applies to broad evaluative words such as robust, strong, solid, clean, direct, sharp, smooth, heavy, light, deep, and rich. A literal-looking Chinese adjective may be understandable but still be the wrong collocation for the intended meaning.

## 2. Treat collocation as a unit

Check whether the verb-object, adjective-noun, adverb-verb, and predicate-subject combination is conventional in Chinese, not merely whether each individual word is correct.

When a phrase feels unusual, ask:
- Would native Chinese prose commonly pair these two words in this meaning?
- Is the expression natural in this register, or only understandable by analogy?
- Is there a more established verb or predicate for this noun?

Prefer a conventional collocation over a semantically literal one. Do not replace a precise technical term merely to sound colloquial.

## 3. Avoid abstract adjective shortcuts when the relation is underspecified

Short predicates such as “更稳、更强、更干净、更硬、更轻、更深” can be natural in conversation and professional jargon. They become awkward when they stand in for several possible meanings.

If the judgment matters, state the property:
- “方案更稳” → “这个方案受输入波动的影响更小” / “这个方案的结果更稳定” / “这个方案实施风险更低”, depending on meaning.
- “论证更强” → “论证更有说服力” / “证据更充分”.
- “表达更干净” → “表达更清晰” / “语言更简练”, depending on meaning.

Do not expand a short predicate when the surrounding context already makes its meaning obvious.

## 4. Prefer Chinese verbal organization when nominalization adds distance

English often packages actions as nouns. Chinese can nominalize too, especially in formal writing, but repeated nominalization easily makes prose static and translated.

Watch for chains such as:
- “对 X 的分析 / 对 Y 的讨论 / 对 Z 的实现”
- “进行……的研究 / 开展……的评估 / 实现……的优化”

When a direct verbal sentence is natural, prefer it. Keep nominalization when the noun itself is the topic, a technical concept, or necessary for formal structure.

## 5. Do not over-specify subjects and pronouns

Chinese often omits a subject or pronoun when it is recoverable from context. Do not repeat “它、他们、该方法、该模型、本文、本研究” in every sentence merely because an English-like sentence frame expects an explicit subject.

At the same time, do not delete a subject when doing so creates ambiguity. The goal is discourse continuity, not maximal omission.

## 6. Break up overloaded “的” modifiers

A long chain of pre-nominal modifiers can be grammatical but hard to process. If the reader must hold too much information before reaching the head noun, unfold part of the modifier into a clause or a separate sentence.

Risk signal:
“基于……提出的用于……并能够……的模型”

Possible repair:
Name the model first, then explain what it is based on, what it is used for, or what it can do.

Do not break a compact technical noun phrase merely because it contains “的”.

## 7. Do not over-explicitate logical relations

Chinese can express relations through word order and context without marking every step with “因此、此外、然而、与此同时、换言之”. Translation and model-generated prose often make these relations more explicit than necessary.

Keep a connective when it prevents ambiguity, marks a real turn, or helps a long argument. Remove it when adjacent sentences already make the relation obvious.

This rule is about frequency and necessity, not about banning connectives.

## 8. Prefer an active, topic-based, or subjectless Chinese sentence when passive wording is only structural

Do not copy an English passive merely to sound objective. Chinese often expresses the same information more naturally with an active sentence, a topic-comment structure, or a subjectless construction.

Keep “被、由……所……” when the affected object is genuinely the focus, the agent is unknown or irrelevant, or the formal register benefits from that construction.

## 9. Avoid stacked relational shells

Phrases such as “在……层面、在……维度、在……方面、从……角度来看、基于……视角” are useful when they distinguish real analytical dimensions. They become translated or bureaucratic when used only to hold a simple statement together.

Prefer the underlying relation when no contrast among dimensions is needed.

## 10. Let genre override anti-translationese heuristics

Scientific, legal, technical, and administrative Chinese may legitimately use explicit subjects, nominalization, passive constructions, long noun phrases, and connectives more often than casual prose. Do not rewrite these structures merely because they are statistically associated with translated Chinese.

Ask whether the construction serves precision, genre convention, or information structure in the current sentence.

## 11. Native-plausibility pass

After drafting, review suspicious phrases as whole units rather than word by word:

1. What exact meaning is this phrase carrying here?
2. Is this a conventional Chinese way to express that meaning in this register?
3. Did a broad foreign-language concept pull me toward a literal adjective, noun, or sentence frame?
4. Would removing an explicit pronoun, connective, nominalization, passive, or long modifier make the sentence more natural without losing precision?
5. If there are several natural Chinese options, choose by meaning and register, not by a fixed replacement table.

When unsure, prefer a plain, established Chinese expression over a novel metaphorical extension created only because it is possible in English.
