# Natural Chinese, native reconstruction, and register consistency

Use this reference when a draft is accurate and reasonably concise but still feels stiff, translated, over-compressed, tonally inconsistent, or oddly polished.

## 1. Naturalness is not minimal word count

Concise Chinese is not the shortest Chinese. Keep words that carry a useful rhetorical function: comparison, qualification, stance, transition, temporal order, emphasis, or cadence.

Less natural in ordinary discussion:
“STE 的出发点更稳。”

More natural:
“相比之下，以 STE 作为出发点会更稳妥一些。”

More informative when the reason matters:
“相比之下，我更倾向于从 STE 出发，因为它约束的是表达本身，受模型口癖变化的影响也更小。”

Choose the shortest form that still sounds complete and natural in context.

## 2. Prefer native reconstruction to local synonym replacement

When a sentence is understandable but still feels unlike native Chinese, do not assume the problem is one bad word. Reconstruct the sentence from its intended meaning.

A good native reconstruction may change:
- word order;
- clause boundaries;
- the main verb;
- the comparison frame;
- the placement of qualifications;
- whether a noun phrase becomes a clause;
- whether a connective is explicit or implicit.

The target is not lexical fidelity. The target is semantic fidelity expressed through a Chinese sentence that feels as if it was originally composed in Chinese.

Example pattern:
“这个表达更干净” should not automatically become “这个表达更清晰”. First decide whether the intended meaning is clarity, brevity, economy, reduced clutter, or sharper focus. Then write the whole sentence accordingly.

## 3. Treat collocation as part of meaning

A phrase can be grammatically legal and still feel wrong because the words do not normally combine that way in Chinese.

When choosing between alternatives, ask which expression a native writer would naturally reach for in this register. Prefer established combinations such as “更稳妥、论证更有说服力、措辞更简练、关系更清楚” when they match the intended sense.

Do not turn this into a fixed replacement table. The same source-language word may need different Chinese expressions in different contexts.

## 4. Keep functional redundancy

Some elements do not add a new fact but still make Chinese easier to follow. Comparison frames, modal softening, conjunction pairs, and short transitions can be useful.

Examples include:
- “相比之下” when two approaches are genuinely being compared;
- “不过” when the argument turns;
- “固然……却……” when conceding one point before limiting it;
- “从这个角度看” when the perspective itself matters.

Do not strip these out merely because the proposition remains logically recoverable. A sentence can be factually intact yet rhetorically abrupt.

## 5. Read for cadence inside the sentence

Natural Chinese prose often relies on the balance between short and long clauses, paired conjunctions, pauses, and the order in which information is released. Long sentences are not inherently worse than short ones.

A compressed sentence can preserve every fact and still lose rhythm. Conversely, a polished sentence can become mannered if every paragraph uses the same contrast or cadence pattern.

Vary sentence shape according to the thought. Do not manufacture variation for its own sake.

## 6. Watch for compressed predicate shorthand

Chinese conversation and professional jargon can use concise predicates such as “更稳、更轻、更硬、更顺、更干净”. They are not wrong. Problems arise when the model uses them as generic evaluative shortcuts across many topics.

Ask what the adjective actually means. If the meaning is important, make it explicit:
- “方案更稳” → “这个方案受输入波动的影响更小” / “实施风险更低” / “结果更稳定”, depending on meaning.
- “表达更干净” → “句子的重点更清楚” / “语言更简练”, depending on meaning.
- “论点很强” → “这个论证更有说服力” / “证据更充分”, depending on meaning.

Keep the short form when the conversational context already makes the meaning obvious.

## 7. Maintain one conversational stance at a time

A discussion can be personal without becoming chatty. A formal explanation can be objective without becoming mechanical.

Avoid sequences like:
“我觉得这个方向挺有意思。STE 的出发点更稳。该方案可降低风格漂移风险。”

The three sentences jump from personal discussion to shorthand judgment to specification language.

A more coherent version might be:
“我觉得这个方向挺有意思。相比之下，我还是更倾向于从 STE 出发，因为它约束的是表达本身，受模型口癖变化的影响也更小。”

For a formal report, remove the conversational stance consistently rather than sentence by sentence.

## 8. Avoid both bureaucratic prose and over-polished commentary

A natural-Chinese pass can overshoot. After removing stiff AI-report language, the model may start writing refined commentary full of “固然、绝非、尤为、归根结底、着眼于、精准、颇具”. These are normal words, but repeated use produces a recognizable editorial manner.

Use polished literary or argumentative phrasing only when the surrounding register supports it. Prefer plain modern Chinese when it expresses the same idea more naturally.

Do not replace one formula with another.

## 9. Do not over-explicitate every relation

Chinese often lets context carry some of the connection between clauses. Keep explicit markers where they genuinely help the reader, but do not label every step as cause, contrast, addition, or summary.

The aim is readable continuity, not maximum logical signage.

## 10. Preserve the user's ownership of the text

When the user has a recognizable voice, aim for text they would plausibly choose to write after careful editing. Do not imitate every spoken filler or typo. Preserve broader traits: how direct they are, how much qualification they prefer, whether they use first person, how long their sentences tend to be, and how formal they want the finished piece to sound.

The standard is not “sounds maximally human”. The standard is “sounds natural, coherent, and appropriate for this author and task”.

## 11. Final native-plausibility pass

Before finalizing a paragraph, ask:
1. If I had never seen the source sentence, would I naturally phrase this idea this way in Chinese?
2. Is the collocation conventional for this meaning and register?
3. Am I preserving a useful rhetorical function, or merely decorating the prose?
4. Does the sentence have enough connective structure to feel complete without becoming over-signposted?
5. Does the paragraph maintain one register and one level of polish?
6. Did I solve the meaning in Chinese, or merely translate the wording into Chinese words?
